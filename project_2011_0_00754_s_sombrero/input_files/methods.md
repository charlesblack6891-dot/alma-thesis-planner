**1. Archive inventory and epoch audit (weeks 1–2)**

Begin at the ALMA Science Archive with project code 2011.0.00754.S. Download the full delivery — raw ASDMs, calibration scripts, QA2 products, and any archive-delivered calibrated measurement sets. Before touching any data, build a spreadsheet with one row per scheduling block: the frequency setting (spectral window central frequencies), the band, the execution date and time (from the ASDM metadata or the archive's execution-block listing), on-source integration time, number of antennas, and the QA2 status. This is your executed-versus-proposed census: the proposal requested 15 frequency settings across Bands 3, 6, 7, and 9, and Cycle 0 programs were routinely only partially observed, so establish exactly which settings exist and are calibratable. Do not assume any setting exists until you see its execution block.

From the same table, compute the epoch spread. Group settings executed within a few days of one another into a single epoch. Any setting executed weeks or months from the main epoch gets flagged now: for those frequencies you will later inflate the flux-density uncertainty by a variability term rather than treat the point as simultaneous with the rest. Estimate that term from the nuclear light curve within the dataset itself where possible — if any frequency setting was observed at two epochs, the flux difference between them is your empirical variability amplitude; otherwise adopt a conservative fractional variability floor and state it explicitly in the error budget. The deliverable of this step is a table you will reproduce verbatim in the thesis: frequency, band, date, integration time, epoch group, and calibration pathway.

Resolve the software pathway in the same two weeks. The delivered reduction scripts target CASA 3.x/4.x. Test three options in order and commit to the first that works: (a) archive-delivered calibrated measurement sets, verified by plotting calibrated visibility amplitudes of the flux and phase calibrators and checking them against the values recorded in the QA2 report; (b) a pinned legacy CASA installation (4.x era) run inside a container or dedicated environment, executing the delivered scripts unmodified; (c) only if both fail, a port of the calibration steps to modern CASA, done one script at a time with amplitude comparisons against the QA2 report at each stage. Record the choice and its verification in your notebook; the whole downstream reduction depends on it being reproducible.

**2. Calibration error budget for slope and curvature (weeks 2–4)**

Before reducing anything, write down the systematic error budget, because it defines what the spectrum can and cannot say. Adopt the Cycle 0 absolute flux-scale accuracies: ~5% in Band 3, ~10% in Bands 6 and 7, and 10–20% in Band 9. Then propagate. For a power law S_ν ∝ ν^α fit between two frequencies ν₁ and ν₂ with fractional flux-scale errors f₁ and f₂, the flux-scale contribution to the index error is σ(α) = sqrt(f₁² + f₂²) / ln(ν₂/ν₁). Over the Band 3–to–Band 7 lever arm (~100–350 GHz, ln(ν₂/ν₁) ≈ 1.25) with 5% and 10% scales, this gives σ(α) ≈ 0.09 — call it ±0.1. Do this calculation yourself in a short Python script, then extend it: simulate the full multi-frequency fit by Monte Carlo, drawing per-band flux-scale offsets (correlated within a band — all spectral windows in one execution share a calibration — and independent between bands), adding thermal noise per point, and fitting both a pure power law and a curved model (log-parabola, log S = a + α log ν + β (log ν)²) to each realization. The scatter of the recovered α is your systematic index uncertainty; the scatter of β tells you the minimum detectable curvature.

Two decisions come out of this milestone and get written down before you look at the real spectrum. First, confirm that σ(α) ≈ 0.1 is sufficient for the science test — distinguishing α ≲ −0.5 from α ≳ 0 at several sigma — which it is. Second, if the Monte Carlo shows the curvature term β is undetectable at Cycle 0 systematics, commit now to reporting curvature as an upper limit/consistency statement rather than a measurement, and let the slope carry the science case. Fixing this in advance is what makes the later interpretation honest.

**3. Calibration of the data (the core, multi-week block)**

Proceed band by band, one execution block at a time, along whichever pathway Milestone 1 selected. If you are running the delivered scripts in legacy CASA, do not treat them as a black box: step through them and verify each stage. The stages, in order, are: a priori flagging (shadowed antennas, edge channels, known bad antennas from the observing log); water-vapor-radiometer and system-temperature corrections; bandpass calibration on the bandpass calibrator; gain (amplitude and phase) calibration on the phase calibrator; and absolute flux scaling from the flux calibrator (a solar-system object or grid quasar, per the delivered script). After each stage, inspect with plotms: bandpass solutions should be smooth in frequency, gain phases should be continuous in time between calibrator scans, and the calibrated phase-calibrator amplitudes should be flat and constant. Flag interactively anything the delivered flagging missed — spikes, decorrelated scans, misbehaving antennas — and log every flag you add, with the reason, in a running reduction journal. Re-derive calibration after any significant new flagging.

Cross-check the flux scale independently: for each execution, note the derived flux density of the phase calibrator and compare it against the ALMA calibrator source catalogue entry for that quasar near that date. Agreement within the nominal band accuracy validates the scale; a discrepancy means you revisit the flux calibration for that execution before proceeding. This check uses only ALMA's own calibrator monitoring, which is part of the observatory calibration system, not external photometry.

**4. Nuclear flux-density extraction by visibility-plane fitting**

The nucleus is an unresolved ~100 mJy point source with little contamination, so measure it in the visibility plane, not from images. For each spectral window of each execution: split out the calibrated target data, average in time and frequency to manageable bins, and fit a point-source model with CASA's uvmodelfit (or equivalently a Python fit to the visibilities via casatools), solving for flux density and position. Record the fitted flux, its statistical uncertainty, and the fitted position; the positions should agree across frequencies to within the astrometric tolerance, and a drifting position is a warning sign of calibration problems.

Verify the point-source assumption per band before trusting the fit: plot visibility amplitude versus uv-distance. A point source is flat; any systematic decline at long baselines indicates resolved structure or decoherence, and any rise at short baselines indicates extended contaminating emission (e.g., dust or free-free from the disk). If short-baseline contamination appears at the higher frequencies, refit using a uv-range cut that excludes the shortest baselines, and report both the cut and the flux difference it makes. As a consistency check, also image each setting with tclean, measure the peak with imfit, and confirm agreement with the uv-plane value within errors; the uv-plane number is the one that goes in the science table.

Group fluxes by spectral window and per frequency setting: the four spectral windows within one setting give you an internal mini-spectral-index whose consistency across settings is another calibration check.

**5. Band 9 handling (conditional branch)**

If Band 9 executions exist, treat them separately. Atmospheric phase decoherence at ~700 GHz biases point-source amplitudes low. The ~100 mJy core is bright enough to attempt phase-only self-calibration: image the core, use the model to solve for per-antenna phases on the shortest solution interval that retains adequate signal-to-noise per antenna, apply, and re-fit the point source. Iterate once or twice at most; then, if the amplitude solutions are stable, one cautious amplitude self-cal round with a long solution interval. Report the pre-self-cal and post-self-cal flux densities side by side, and carry the difference between them as an additional systematic term in the Band 9 error bar. If self-calibration fails to converge — solutions dominated by noise, or the flux keeps drifting between iterations — drop Band 9 from the quantitative spectral fit entirely and note only its (biased-low) measured value as a lower limit. Nothing in the central analysis depends on this band.

**6. Assembling the error-budgeted spectrum**

Build the master table, one row per frequency: frequency, band, epoch, flux density, and three separately listed uncertainty components — thermal/fit statistical error, flux-scale systematic (per the Milestone 2 budget, correlated within band), and the variability term for any non-simultaneous setting from the Milestone 1 audit (plus the self-cal term for Band 9). This table, with all components shown rather than a single collapsed error bar, is deliverable 1 and should appear in full in the thesis.

**7. Spectral index and curvature fitting**

Fit the spectrum in log S–log ν space with a likelihood that respects the error structure: statistical errors independent per point, flux-scale errors fully correlated within a band and independent between bands (implement via per-band nuisance scale factors with Gaussian priors set to the Cycle 0 accuracies, marginalized either by MCMC with emcee or by direct optimization plus the Milestone 2 Monte Carlo). Fit two models: (i) a single power law, yielding α ± σ(α) with the systematic floor from Milestone 2; (ii) the log-parabola, yielding the curvature β. Compare the two fits with a likelihood-ratio or information-criterion test whose detection threshold you calibrated in the Milestone 2 simulations — curvature is claimed only if it exceeds what flux-scale systematics alone can mimic in those simulations. If the curved fit prefers a turnover inside the observed range, quote the peak frequency with its uncertainty from the posterior; if not, quote the constraint the data place on any peak within 100–350 GHz. Repeat the power-law fit excluding Band 9 (if present) and excluding any variability-flagged points, and show that α is stable against these cuts; the baseline result is the Bands 3–7 fit.

**8. Confrontation with the model families**

The interpretation follows the asymmetric decision logic fixed at the outset, applied at the qualitative spectral-shape level with no radiative-transfer fitting. Write the logic as an explicit decision table before unblinding the fitted α. If α ≲ −0.5 at the budgeted significance: the spectrum is optically thin and steep, which is inconsistent with the flat-to-inverted, rising synchrotron spectrum of an ADAF-type hot accretion flow at these frequencies, and indicates optically thin jet emission dominates the millimeter core — a decisive statement, quoted with the full systematic error bar. If α is flat to inverted (α ≳ −0.2): report that the shape is consistent with both an ADAF and a partially self-absorbed compact jet core, and make the degeneracy quantitative using your own measurements — state the measured α and the curvature/peak constraint from step 7, and identify, from the qualitative shapes of the two model families, which spectral feature (curvature sign, peak-out frequency location) each family predicts and where your constraint sits relative to each. Intermediate α values are reported as such, with the confidence interval straddling the decision boundary stated plainly. In every branch, the claim is a statement about spectral shape backed by the error budget of Milestone 2, and every number in the discussion traces back to the master table of step 6.

**9. Reproducibility packaging (final weeks)**

Throughout, keep the entire workflow in version control: the reduction journal, all CASA scripts or ported equivalents, the flag lists, the fitting code, and the Monte Carlo. The final chapter's figures — the uv-amplitude checks, the spectrum with per-component error bars, and the fit posteriors — must regenerate from the master table with a single script, so that anyone rerunning your pipeline from the archived delivery reproduces the thesis numbers end to end.

**Step 1: Assemble the archival line dataset.**
Begin by building a single, homogeneous spreadsheet (or lightweight database, e.g. an astropy Table saved to CSV/ECSV) of every published single-dish measurement toward clump G. The primary source is Turner, Xu & Rieu (1992) and the handful of related single-dish papers on IC 443G's shock chemistry; pull, for each reported transition of CO, HCO+, HCN, CN, SiO, CS, SO, and H2CO, the peak/integrated intensity (main-beam or antenna temperature, as originally reported — record which), the LSR velocity, the FWHM linewidth, and the telescope beam size at that frequency. Do the same for the broad-line [CI] detection. Where a paper reports both a narrow (~a few km/s) and a broad (~10 km/s) velocity component at the same position, keep these as separate rows rather than blending them — this narrow/broad split is the observational basis for the later two-shock-component test. Convert all intensities to a common flux/brightness convention and, where beam sizes differ between transitions/telescopes, apply a simple beam-dilution correction so that all measurements refer to a consistent solid angle before ratios are taken. Cross-check transcribed numbers directly against the tables/figures in the source papers (not secondary compilations) and keep a provenance column (paper, table number) for every entry so the fit results can be traced back to their input.

**Step 2: Derive column densities and densities via non-LTE excitation analysis.**
For each species with more than one observed transition (or, where only one transition exists, using it in conjunction with standard assumed abundance/excitation constraints), run RADEX (or an equivalent publicly available non-LTE radiative transfer code) to solve for the volume density (n(H2)) and column density that reproduce the observed line intensities and ratios. Do this separately for the narrow and broad velocity components identified in Step 1, since they likely trace different gas (undisturbed/ambient vs. shocked). Use collisional rate coefficients from the LAMDA database for each molecule. Treat kinetic temperature as a free parameter within the range spectroscopically justified by the literature (e.g., from CO line ratios or ancillary studies of IC 443), and run a grid of RADEX models over density and column density at fixed temperature steps, identifying the best-fit (χ²-minimizing) solution and its uncertainty region for each species/component. Tabulate the resulting n(H2), N(species), and where possible fractional abundances relative to N(H2) (using the CO column as a total-gas proxy with a standard CO abundance) for both velocity components. This step converts the raw line list from Step 1 into a physically interpretable set of density/column-density constraints that can be compared to shock-model predictions.

**Step 3: Fit single-shock-type models to the derived abundances.**
Obtain a publicly available grid of parametrized shock-chemistry models — e.g., outputs from the Paris-Durham shock code (C-shock and J-shock families) or a published tabulation of Flower & Pineau des Forêts-type grids — spanning shock velocity, pre-shock density, and magnetic-field scaling parameter. For each species' abundance (or abundance ratio, e.g., SiO/CO, HCO+/CO, CN/HCN, SO/CO, H2CO/CO) derived in Step 2, compare the observed value against the model grid's predicted abundance as a function of shock parameters, separately for the C-type grid and the J-type grid. Perform a grid-search fit (minimizing χ² summed over all species) to identify the best-fit single C-shock model and the best-fit single J-shock model that most closely reproduce the full set of observed abundances/ratios simultaneously. Record the per-species residuals for each fit — this species-by-species residual pattern is what will reveal whether one shock type alone is adequate or systematically fails for particular tracers (e.g., SiO commonly requires higher shock velocities than CN or HCO+).

**Step 4: Fit a two-component (mixed C+J) shock model and compare.**
Construct a mixture model in which the observed clump-integrated abundance of each species is a weighted linear combination of a C-shock-grid abundance and a J-shock-grid abundance (weights representing the beam-filling fraction of each shock type, plus the shock parameters of each component as additional free parameters). Fit this mixture model to the same abundance/ratio dataset used in Step 3, allowing the C- and J-shock parameters and their relative weight to vary. Use the narrow/broad velocity decomposition from Step 2 as an independent physical anchor for the mixture fit where possible (e.g., associating the broad, high-velocity component preferentially with J-shock or high-velocity C-shock chemistry, and the narrow component with lower-velocity C-shock or ambient gas), rather than treating the two shock components as a free-floating statistical fit alone.

**Step 5: Statistical model comparison.**
Quantitatively compare the single-C-shock, single-J-shock, and mixed C+J-shock fits using a formal goodness-of-fit criterion that penalizes the added free parameters of the mixture model — e.g., reduced χ², AIC, or BIC, and/or a Bayesian evidence ratio if a Bayesian fitting framework (such as an MCMC sampler like emcee applied to the shock-grid interpolation) is adopted for the parameter search. Determine whether the mixture model's improvement in fit is statistically justified given its additional degrees of freedom, or whether a single shock type — potentially with a broader allowed parameter range — is statistically sufficient to explain the data. Present this comparison explicitly, species by species and in aggregate, as the thesis's central quantitative result.

**Step 6: Synthesize and present best-fit shock parameters.**
Report the best-fit shock velocity, pre-shock density, and (if constrained) magnetic-field scaling factor for whichever model(s) are statistically preferred, with uncertainties propagated from the grid-search/MCMC posterior. Present these alongside the RADEX-derived densities and column densities from Step 2 as a self-consistent physical picture of clump G's shocked gas. Frame the final write-up as a quantitative test of the existing qualitative literature claim (that a J+C shock mixture is required), explicitly stating whether the reanalysis confirms, refines, or fails to statistically require that mixture, and reporting the best-fit shock parameters for each surviving model as the thesis's primary result. All software used (RADEX, the shock-model grid, and any fitting/plotting code) should be run and documented in a reproducible script or notebook (e.g., Python with astropy, a RADEX wrapper such as SpectralRadex, and standard grid-interpolation/least-squares or MCMC libraries) so that the full compiled dataset, RADEX runs, and shock-model fits can be regenerated and checked.

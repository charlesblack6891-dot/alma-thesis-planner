**Summary for a beginner:** Massive, hot stars blow powerful winds that carry material away into space, and current estimates of how fast they lose that mass may be wrong by as much as a factor of ten because nobody knows whether the wind is smooth or made of dense "clumps." This project uses ALMA's radio-brightness measurements of one massive star, taken at two or three different wavelengths, to test whether the wind looks smooth or clumpy — a diagnostic that helps pin down the star's true mass-loss rate.

**Scope check:** The proposal talks about combining ALMA data with information "from the Far-UV to radio domain" inside an "elaborate Non-LTE wind model" — that full multi-wavelength modeling effort is far beyond a single thesis and will not be attempted. The benchmark used to judge "smooth vs. clumpy" (a spectral index of α≈0.6 for free-free emission from a spherically symmetric, constant-velocity, smooth wind) is not stated in the data description; it comes from standard massive-star wind theory external to this proposal, and is only as reliable as the idealized assumptions behind it — real winds can depart from spherical symmetry or constant terminal velocity (rotation, asymmetries, binarity) in ways that mimic or hide clumping, and this thesis will not attempt to disentangle those effects. The stellar parameters (distance, terminal velocity, radius) needed to turn a spectral index into an absolute mass-loss rate are not part of the data description and must come from the literature if attempted at all; this makes the mass-loss-rate number a secondary, contingent stretch goal, not the guaranteed deliverable. Finally, the proposal describes bands that were *requested*, not confirmed delivered at SNR ≥ 10 in two or more bands for any specific star — this must be verified against the actual calibrated products before any science proceeds, with no archival fallback, since the data description names no external catalog and radio/mm emission from OB winds is known to vary intrinsically on month-to-year timescales.

**Step 1 — Feasibility gate.** Before choosing a target, survey the delivered, calibrated continuum images and visibility data for this project. For every OB-type star observed, record which ALMA band(s) were actually observed and reduced, and measure the continuum SNR of each source in each band's calibrated image (peak flux over local rms noise in an emission-free region of the same image). Retain only stars with SNR ≥ 10 — the project's own stated detection threshold — in at least two separate bands. If no star clears this bar, stop and select a different ALMA project; do not substitute an archival flux point from an unnamed external source, since the data description does not point to any specific catalog and non-simultaneous OB-wind flux measurements are not safely comparable due to intrinsic wind variability. Document this feasibility check itself as the first analysis step of the thesis, not as a background assumption.

**Step 2 — Target and band selection.** From the stars passing Step 1, select a single target for the full analysis — pick whichever qualifying star has the cleanest field (least contamination from nearby sources, no resolved structure that would complicate point-source measurement) and the largest number of qualifying bands (two or three, matching what the project observed). Note explicitly whether the retained bands were observed simultaneously or separated in time, since any spectral index computed from non-simultaneous bands must be flagged as potentially affected by intrinsic wind variability rather than purely by wind structure.

**Step 3 — Flux density measurement.** For the chosen target, measure the continuum flux density in each retained band directly from the calibrated CASA images (or, if a cleaner measurement is warranted, from the calibrated visibility data). Since OB winds are expected to be compact and largely unresolved at ALMA resolution, use point-source fitting (e.g., a 2D Gaussian or delta-function fit convolved with the synthesized beam, via CASA's `imfit`) as the primary method; cross-check with simple aperture photometry (integrating flux in a beam-sized aperture centered on the source, with local background subtraction) as a consistency test. Record the flux density and its uncertainty (combining image rms noise and, where available, the absolute flux calibration uncertainty for each band, typically of order 5–10% for ALMA) for every retained band.

**Step 4 — Spectral index determination.** Using only the two or three flux densities measured from this project's own ALMA bands (do not mix in flux from any other facility or archival source), fit S_ν ∝ ν^α by a linear regression in log(S_ν) versus log(ν), propagating the flux uncertainties from Step 3 into a formal uncertainty on α. If only two bands are available, compute α and its uncertainty directly from the two-point log-log slope. Report the band center frequencies as used in the observations (observed-frame values as delivered in the calibrated data) rather than converting to any other frame, since the data description does not specify redshift or rest-frame corrections for this Galactic target.

**Step 5 — Model comparison and classification.** Compare the measured α (with its uncertainty) against the α≈0.6 benchmark for a smooth, spherically symmetric, constant-velocity wind, making clear in the write-up that this benchmark is an external idealized theoretical value, not something specified in the proposal. Classify the result as: consistent with the smooth-wind value within uncertainties; or significantly flatter/steeper than 0.6 in the direction predicted by radially stratified clumping models (state which direction and by how much, in units of the propagated uncertainty). Discuss explicitly, as part of this same result, the confounds identified in the scope check — non-spherical geometry, non-constant terminal velocity, possible non-simultaneity of the bands — as alternative explanations that cannot be ruled out with this dataset alone. This classification, with its stated uncertainty and caveats, is the thesis's primary deliverable.

**Step 6 — Optional stretch goal: absolute mass-loss rate.** Only if literature values for the star's distance, terminal wind velocity, and stellar/wind radius can be located, propagate the measured α (and its uncertainty) through the standard radio mass-loss-rate formalism for free-free wind emission (the Wright & Barlow / Panagia & Felli type relation linking radio flux, spectral index, and mass-loss rate) to derive an approximate mass-loss rate and an approximate clumping factor relative to the smooth-wind case. Propagate uncertainties from both the measured α and the adopted literature stellar parameters into the final quoted range. Label this result throughout as contingent on external literature values not supplied by the data description, and as lower-confidence than the Step 5 classification.
